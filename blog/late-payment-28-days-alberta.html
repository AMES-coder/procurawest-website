What happens if you don't pay a contractor within 28 days in Alberta.

Amy-Marie Holland November 19, 2026 6 min read

Alberta's Prompt Payment and Construction Lien Act gives you 28 days to pay a proper invoice. Not 28 business days. Not 28 days after your internal approval process finishes. Twenty-eight calendar days from the date you receive a proper invoice - and when that clock runs out, the consequences start stacking fast.

I have covered the owner's obligations under the PPCLA and the full breakdown of PPCLA payment deadlines in earlier posts. This one focuses specifically on what happens when you miss the 28-day window - day by day, consequence by consequence.

Day 29: Interest starts automatically

There is no warning letter. No grace period. No courtesy call from the contractor reminding you the invoice is overdue. On day 29, interest begins accruing on the unpaid amount at the Bank of Canada overnight rate plus 2%, compounded monthly.

The contractor does not need to send you a notice. They do not need to demand payment. The interest obligation is statutory - it is triggered by the calendar, not by a letter.

~6.75% Approximate annual interest rate At a Bank of Canada overnight rate of 4.75% plus the statutory 2%, a $500,000 invoice accrues roughly $92 per day in interest starting on day 29. Over 60 days late, that is $5,500 in interest alone - money you owe on top of the original invoice, with no negotiation required.

That interest rate moves with the Bank of Canada rate, so it changes over time. But the formula is always the same: Bank of Canada overnight rate + 2%, compounded monthly. It is not negotiable and it cannot be contracted out of. Even if your contract says "net 60" or "net 90," the PPCLA overrides those terms for construction work in Alberta.

Day 29 to 35: The contractor can issue a notice of non-payment

Once the 28-day payment window closes, the contractor has the right to issue a notice of non-payment. This is a formal written notice that puts you on record as late and sets the stage for adjudication.

Here is what a lot of people get wrong about this notice: the contractor does not need to send one before interest accrues. Interest is automatic. The notice of non-payment is the contractor's tool for escalating toward adjudication - it is a step in the dispute resolution process, not a prerequisite for the financial penalty.

Most owners I work with are genuinely surprised when they learn the PPCLA does not require any notice before interest kicks in. They assume they will get a warning. The Act does not work that way.

Day 35 and beyond: Adjudication becomes available

Once the payment dispute exists, the contractor can refer the matter to adjudication under the PPCLA. This is not arbitration. This is not mediation. Adjudication is a fast, binding interim process designed to keep money moving in the construction payment chain.

Here is what that means in practice:

  • Speed. An adjudicator is typically appointed within days of the referral. The process usually concludes within 30 to 50 days. Compare that to litigation, which can take years.
  • Binding on an interim basis. The adjudicator's determination is binding and enforceable immediately. You must comply with the payment order even if you disagree and plan to pursue the matter further in court.
  • Cost exposure. The losing party typically pays the adjudicator's fees. If you are late on a straightforward invoice and the contractor refers it to adjudication, you are likely paying the adjudicator's costs on top of the invoice amount and accrued interest.

Adjudication was designed to protect contractors from exactly the kind of payment delays that used to be standard practice in Alberta construction. It works. And it works fast.

Day 42: The flow-down problem

Here is the part that catches general contractors off guard. Under the PPCLA, when you receive payment from the owner, you have 7 days to pay your contractors. If the owner pays you on day 28 and you take another 14 days to process payment downstream, you are now late on your own statutory obligation.

When you are late paying your prime contractor and they are late paying their contractors as a result, the payment chain breaks. Every party downstream is now dealing with cash flow problems caused by your missed deadline. And every one of them has the same adjudication rights.

One missed 28-day deadline at the top of the chain can trigger multiple adjudication referrals from parties you have never contracted with directly. The PPCLA is designed to prevent this exact scenario - and when it happens anyway, the consequences multiply.

Day 60 and beyond: Lien exposure compounds

The PPCLA works alongside Alberta's construction lien provisions. A contractor who has not been paid can register a lien against the property where the work was performed. The lien rights exist independently of the prompt payment provisions, but a missed PPCLA deadline makes the lien claim stronger - because you are demonstrably in breach of your statutory payment obligation.

By day 60, you are facing accrued interest, potential adjudication costs, possible lien registrations, and a supply chain that has lost confidence in your ability to pay. Contractors talk. Word travels fast in Alberta's construction market, and a reputation for late payment makes it harder to attract competitive bids on future work.

What a notice of non-payment does - and does not - protect

If you have a legitimate dispute with the invoice - the work was defective, the scope was not completed, the amount does not match the contract - you have the right to issue a notice of non-payment within the 28-day window. This notice must be in writing, must identify the invoice, and must state the reason for non-payment or partial payment.

A properly issued notice of non-payment protects you from interest on the disputed portion of the invoice. It does not eliminate the contractor's right to refer the dispute to adjudication. It does not give you unlimited time to resolve the issue. And it only covers the amount you genuinely dispute - if you owe $500,000 and dispute $50,000 of it, you still need to pay the undisputed $450,000 within 28 days.

How to fix a missed deadline

If you have already missed the 28-day window, here is what to do:

  1. Pay immediately. Calculate and include the statutory interest from day 29 through to the date of payment. Do not wait for the contractor to demand it - paying proactively shows good faith and can prevent escalation.
  2. Acknowledge the late payment in writing. Contact the contractor, confirm the interest calculation, and document the corrective action. If the contractor has already issued a notice of non-payment, respond to it formally.
  3. Fix your AP process. Map the 28-day clock to your invoice approval workflow. If your current process takes 35 or 45 days from invoice receipt to payment, you have a structural compliance problem. The PPCLA does not care about your internal approvals - it cares about the calendar.
  4. If adjudication has been filed, pay before the determination. You can still resolve an adjudication referral by paying the full amount plus interest before the adjudicator issues a determination. This avoids adjudicator fees and the precedent of a binding order against you.

The real cost of being late

The financial penalties under the PPCLA are real, but they are not the biggest cost of habitually late payments. The real cost is reputational. Contractors in Alberta have options. The ones you want working on your projects - the safest, most experienced, most reliable firms - will prioritize clients who pay on time. If you are known as a slow payer, you end up with the contractors nobody else wanted.

Fix the process before it costs you a bid, a relationship, or an adjudication order.
