What happens if you don't pay a contractor within 28 days in Alberta.

Amy-Marie Holland November 19, 2026 6 min read

Alberta's Prompt Payment and Construction Lien Act (PPCLA) changed the rules for how construction payments work in this province. The 28-day payment deadline is not a suggestion - it is a statutory requirement with real consequences when you miss it.

Here is what actually happens when that clock runs out, and what you can do about it.

The 28-day rule explained

Under the PPCLA, once an owner receives a proper invoice from a contractor, they have 28 calendar days to either pay the invoice or issue a notice of non-payment explaining what they are disputing and why.

For contractors further down the chain, the timelines are tighter. A general contractor who receives payment from the owner must pay their contractors within 7 days of receiving that payment. The same 7-day flow-down applies at every tier.

These timelines are mandatory. You cannot contract out of them. Even if your agreement says "net 60" or "payment upon approval," the PPCLA overrides those terms.

What happens on day 29

The moment you miss the 28-day deadline without paying or issuing a valid notice of non-payment, three things happen automatically:

BoC + 2% Automatic Interest Rate Interest begins accruing at the Bank of Canada rate plus 2 percent, compounded monthly, from the day after the payment deadline. No demand letter is required - the interest is owed by operation of law.

1. Interest starts accruing. The statutory interest rate is the Bank of Canada rate plus 2 percent, compounded monthly. This is not a penalty that the contractor has to demand - it kicks in automatically. On a $200,000 invoice that is 60 days late, the interest alone can be several thousand dollars.

2. Adjudication rights activate. The contractor now has the right to refer the payment dispute to adjudication under the PPCLA. Adjudication is a fast-track dispute resolution process where an independent adjudicator reviews the evidence and issues a binding interim decision. The process is designed to be completed within weeks, not months.

3. Lien rights are preserved. The contractor's right to register a construction lien against the property remains intact. A lien is a legal claim against the land itself, which can complicate financing, sales, and project completion.

What happens on day 42

By day 42 - two weeks past the deadline - the situation has escalated. The contractor has likely sent a demand letter. Interest continues to compound. If the contractor has referred the dispute to adjudication, the adjudicator may already be reviewing the case.

At this stage, paying the full amount plus interest is still your cleanest exit. Every day you wait strengthens the contractor's position and weakens yours.

What happens on day 60 and beyond

By day 60, you are facing a combination of compounding interest, a potential adjudication decision ordering immediate payment, and the real possibility of a construction lien registered against the property. If the contractor has contractors of their own who have not been paid because of the delay, you may be dealing with multiple parties and multiple claims.

The PPCLA was designed to end the culture of "pay when paid" in construction. The consequences for late payment are intentionally steep because the old system allowed owners and general contractors to hold payment for months without consequence.

The flow-down impact

Late payment does not stop with the contractor you owe. Under the PPCLA, payment flows down through the entire chain. When you do not pay the general contractor on time, the GC cannot pay their contractors on time, and those contractors cannot pay their suppliers.

This creates a cascade of consequences:

  • Multiple parties accruing interest charges against the payment chain.
  • Contractors at lower tiers exercising their own adjudication rights.
  • Potential work slowdowns or stoppages as unpaid contractors protect their position.
  • Damage to your reputation in a market where word travels fast among contractors.

What a notice of non-payment does (and does not) do

If you have a legitimate dispute with the invoice, the PPCLA gives you a tool: the notice of non-payment. This is a written notice that must be delivered within the 28-day payment period, explaining which portions of the invoice you are disputing and the reasons for the dispute.

What it does:

  • Preserves your right to dispute the specific amounts identified in the notice.
  • Stops interest from accruing on the disputed portion (if the dispute is valid).
  • Creates a formal record of your position if the matter goes to adjudication.

What it does not do:

  • It does not give you the right to withhold the entire invoice. You must pay the undisputed portion on time.
  • It does not prevent the contractor from referring the dispute to adjudication.
  • It does not extend the payment deadline for the undisputed amounts.

A notice of non-payment is not a free pass to hold funds. It is a mechanism for handling genuine disputes while keeping the rest of the payment chain moving. For a full breakdown of owner obligations under the PPCLA, see my PPCLA owner's guide.

How to fix a missed deadline

If you have already missed the 28-day window, here is what to do:

  1. Pay the undisputed amount immediately - do not wait for the contractor to send a demand letter. Every day adds interest.
  2. Calculate and include the statutory interest - pay it proactively. This shows good faith and may prevent escalation.
  3. If you dispute part of the invoice, issue a notice of non-payment for the disputed portion even if you are past the 28-day mark. It is better late than never for documenting your position.
  4. Communicate directly with the contractor - explain the situation, confirm your payment plan, and put everything in writing.
  5. Fix your internal process - set up calendar reminders for payment deadlines and build invoice review into your weekly workflow so this does not happen again.

Prevention is cheaper than interest

The easiest way to avoid PPCLA consequences is to build payment deadline tracking into your procurement process from day one. Track every invoice receipt date, calculate the 28-day deadline, and start your review immediately - not when someone remembers to look at it.
