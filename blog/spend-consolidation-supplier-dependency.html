Spend consolidation without supplier lock-in: how to get the savings without creating a monopoly.

Amy-Marie Holland October 26, 2026 8 min read

Consolidating spend is one of the first things I look at in any procurement review. Forty vendors doing the same type of work means forty sets of rates, forty insurance certificates to track, forty invoices a month to check and no real buying power with any of them. Whether you're running oil and gas projects, construction, manufacturing, facilities or any operation with a long vendor list, it's often the fastest cost reduction available. Cut that down to a handful of strong suppliers and you get better pricing, less admin and suppliers who actually care about keeping your business.

But there's a point where consolidation stops saving money and starts costing it. Go too far and one vendor ends up owning the category. They know you have nowhere else to go. Rates creep up, service slips, and the next time you try to bid the work out, nobody else is qualified, set up on site or interested.

The goal isn't fewer suppliers. It's the right number of suppliers, with you still in control of the relationship.

What spend consolidation gets you

  • Better pricing. More volume with one supplier gives you a real reason to ask for volume rates, rebates or price holds.
  • Less admin. Fewer vendor setups, fewer prequalification files, fewer invoices and fewer contracts to renew.
  • Cleaner compliance. It's easier to keep COR, WCB clearance and insurance current for 8 vendors than for 40.
  • Stronger relationships. A supplier that does a meaningful share of your work will prioritize your call-outs, your shutdowns and your problems.
  • Better data. Spend is easier to see and benchmark when it isn't scattered across dozens of vendors and one-off POs.

How a supplier monopoly creeps in

Nobody sets out to create supplier dependency. It happens one convenient decision at a time: a sole-source award because the timeline was tight, an MSA that auto-renews because nobody had time to re-bid it, a second vendor dropped because the primary was "doing fine." A few years later the category has one supplier and no plan B.

Warning signs you're already there

Rate increases get accepted without a benchmark. There's no second qualified vendor set up in your system. The supplier holds your drawings, site knowledge or equipment and you couldn't easily take them back. Contracts auto-renew without a market check. Your team says "we can't switch, they're the only ones who know the site." And the supplier stops negotiating, because they don't need to.

Dependency runs both ways, too. If your business makes up a very large share of a small supplier's revenue, you carry their risk as well. If they lose a key person, hit a cash crunch or close, your operation feels it immediately.

Seven controls that keep consolidation working for you

01

Segment before you consolidate

Not every category should be consolidated the same way. Low-risk, routine spend like PPE, consumables and office supplies can go to one supplier with little downside. Critical categories, such as anything that can shut down production, delay a turnaround or has very few qualified providers, need a backup no matter how good the primary is.

02

Use a primary and secondary supplier

For critical categories, award most of the work to a primary supplier and keep a real share with a secondary. The split is up to you, but the secondary needs enough work to stay set up, stay familiar with your sites and stay interested. A backup vendor that hasn't worked for you in two years isn't a backup.

03

Keep alternates prequalified

Even if you don't use them often, keep two or three alternates current in ISN, Avetta or your own prequalification process. When you need to switch, the slowest part is usually onboarding, not finding the vendor.

04

Write the exit into the contract

Your MSA should protect you if the relationship goes bad. Look for termination for convenience, no exclusivity language, ownership of your drawings, data and site documentation, and a transition assistance clause that requires the supplier to hand over cleanly if you move the work.

05

Control how rates move

A consolidated supplier with open-ended rate increases is the most expensive kind of supplier. Tie escalation to a published index with a cap, set a fixed notice period for rate changes, and add a benchmarking clause that lets you compare their rates to the market.

06

Re-bid on a schedule

Take major categories to market every few years, even if you expect to keep the incumbent. A real competitive process resets pricing, reminds the incumbent that the work isn't guaranteed and keeps your knowledge of the market current. Put the re-bid dates in your contract calendar so they don't get skipped.

07

Track concentration

Once a quarter, look at what share of each category's spend sits with each supplier. Set your own threshold for when a category is too concentrated and needs a second source developed. If you can't pull that report today, that's the first gap to fix.

The Alberta reality: thin markets

In parts of Alberta, especially during turnaround season or in remote locations, there may only be one or two qualified contractors for a scope. That isn't a reason to give up on competition. It's a reason to plan for it. Develop your secondary suppliers before peak season, not during it. Document why a category is sole-sourced, and set a date to revisit it. I wrote about how to document sole-source decisions in an earlier post.

Consolidation is a savings strategy. Dependency is a risk you didn't plan for. The difference is whether you could still walk away.

A simple test

For each major category, ask yourself one question: if this supplier raised rates 20% tomorrow, or couldn't show up for your next shutdown, what would you do? If the honest answer is "pay it" or "nothing," that category needs a second source, better contract terms or both.

Common questions about supplier consolidation

What is spend consolidation?

Spend consolidation means moving purchases in a category from many suppliers to fewer, so you buy in larger volumes, get better pricing and spend less time managing vendors. It usually starts with a spend analysis that shows who you're buying from, how much and how often.

How many suppliers should I have per category?

There's no single right number. Routine, low-risk categories can often go to one supplier. Critical categories, where a supplier failure would stop work or delay a shutdown, should have at least a primary and a qualified secondary.

What is supplier dependency risk?

Supplier dependency risk is what happens when one vendor becomes so embedded in your operation that you can't easily replace them. You lose negotiating power on rates, and you're exposed if that supplier has a safety, quality, capacity or financial problem.

When should I bring in a procurement consultant?

When you know you're spending too much with too many vendors but don't have the time to pull the data, run the bids and renegotiate the contracts yourself. Contract procurement support gets the work done without adding a full-time hire.
