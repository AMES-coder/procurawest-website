How to audit contractor invoices in Alberta: a practical guide.

Amy-Marie Holland November 19, 2026 7 min read

Most contractor invoices get approved the same way: someone in accounts payable glances at the total, checks whether the PO number matches, and clicks approve. If the amount looks roughly right - close to what was expected - it goes through.

That's not an audit. That's pattern recognition. And it misses the billing errors that actually cost money - wrong rates applied, hours that don't match field records, materials billed to the wrong job, change order scope invoiced without authorization.

A proper invoice audit isn't about suspecting fraud. It's about verifying that what you're paying matches what you agreed to pay. Here's the process I use.

The 7-step audit process

Step 1

Pull the contract before you open the invoice

This sounds obvious, but most billing errors get approved because nobody checked the contract. Before you review a single line item, pull up: the contract or subcontract, the current rate schedule (including any amendments or escalation adjustments), the scope of work, and any active change orders. You need these documents open side by side with the invoice. If you're reviewing from memory, you're guessing.

Step 2

Verify every rate against the rate schedule

Go line by line. Check every labour rate, equipment rate, and unit rate on the invoice against the contracted rate schedule. This includes checking the labour classification - a lead hand rate billed for a journeyman position is a common error. If the contract has had rate amendments (annual escalation, for example), make sure the invoice is using the current rates, not last year's or next year's. Document any discrepancies with the specific contract rate reference.

Step 3

Cross-reference hours against field records

For T&M or reimbursable contracts, compare the invoiced hours against whatever field records you have: site access logs, daily labour reports, supervisor daily counts, or signed field tickets. You're looking for hours billed that exceed what was recorded on site. Even a one-hour-per-person-per-day discrepancy on a 10-person crew over a month adds up to 200+ hours of unbacked billing. If you don't have field records to compare against, that's a process gap worth fixing.

Step 4

Verify materials and equipment charges

For cost-plus or reimbursable contracts, materials should be supported by supplier invoices showing the contractor's actual cost. Check that the markup matches the contracted percentage. For equipment, verify rental rates against third-party rental invoices (if rented) or the contractor's internal rate schedule (if contractor-owned). Watch for equipment billed on days when no work was performed - if the crew was stood down but the crane is still on the invoice, that's a question worth asking.

Step 5

Confirm every charge ties back to authorized scope

Every line item on the invoice should map to either the original scope of work or a signed change order. If a charge doesn't fit either, it's unauthorized. This is particularly important for "extras" or "additional scope" that the contractor performed but that were never formally authorized through a change order request (COR). The work may have been done - but without authorization, the charge doesn't have a contractual basis.

Step 6

Compare against prior invoicing periods

Pull the last two or three invoices from the same contractor and compare them side by side. You're looking for: duplicate charges that appear on this invoice and a prior one, charges that have been increasing without a scope change, and patterns like consistently round numbers on hours or quantities (which may indicate estimated rather than actual billing). Duplicate billing across periods is more common than most organizations expect - especially on long-running contracts with monthly invoicing.

Step 7

Verify the math

Check the extended pricing (rate times quantity), subtotals, GST calculation, and holdback deduction. Recalculate the total from the line items up. If the total doesn't match the sum of the line items, that's either a calculation error or an intentional padding. Also verify the holdback calculation - it should be 10% of the value of work done under the PPCLA, applied to the correct base amount.

What to do when you find something

When an audit reveals a discrepancy, the response depends on the type and severity:

Rate errors: Send the invoice back with the specific discrepancy noted - "Line 4 bills $85/hr for Electrician Journeyman; contracted rate per Schedule A is $78/hr." Request a revised invoice. Under the PPCLA, you should issue a notice of non-payment within 14 days on the disputed amount and pay the undisputed balance within 28 days.

Unauthorized scope: If charges relate to work not covered by the contract or a signed change order, withhold that amount and document the reason in your notice of non-payment. If the work was actually performed and has value, work with the contractor to formalize it through a change order - but don't approve the charge without one.

Duplicate charges: Flag the duplicate with references to both invoices. Request a credit on the next invoice or a revised current invoice. If the same pattern appears repeatedly, it may indicate a systemic billing process issue on the contractor's end that warrants a conversation beyond the individual invoice.

Pattern issues across multiple invoices: If you're seeing the same types of errors across several invoicing periods - consistently inflated hours, regularly elevated classifications, recurring unauthorized charges - that's a contract compliance conversation, not just an invoice correction. Consider a full contract compliance review that covers the entire invoicing history.

Building a sustainable process

A one-time audit catches what's already on the invoice. A repeatable process prevents the same errors from recurring. Here's what I recommend:

  • Create an audit template for each contract. Pre-populate it with the contracted rates, authorized scope, and invoicing requirements so the reviewer has everything they need without pulling the contract fresh each time.
  • Set audit thresholds. Every invoice gets a basic check (rates, math, scope). Invoices over a certain dollar threshold get the full 7-step audit. Determine the threshold based on your risk tolerance and the volume of invoices you process.
  • Track discrepancies over time. Log every error you find - contractor, type of error, amount, invoice period. After six months, you'll see patterns that tell you which contractors need closer scrutiny and which contract areas are generating the most billing issues.
  • Brief your field teams. The people on site are your best source of information for hours and attendance verification. Make sure your site supervisors and PMs understand that their daily labour counts and field records are part of the invoice audit trail.
The goal of an invoice audit isn't to find fraud. It's to verify that what you're paying matches what you agreed to pay. Most discrepancies are honest errors - but honest errors cost the same as dishonest ones.

When to bring in outside help

If you don't have the internal capacity to audit invoices systematically - or if you suspect there's a significant billing problem across a portfolio of contracts - an external contract compliance review can cover the gap. I typically start with the highest-value or highest-risk contracts, audit the full invoicing history, quantify the recoverable amount, and build the audit process that prevents the same issues going forward.
