PPCLA payment deadlines in Alberta: what every contractor needs to know.

Amy-Marie Holland November 19, 2026 7 min read

Alberta's Prompt Payment and Construction Lien Act (PPCLA) has been in force since August 29, 2022. It replaced the old Builders' Lien Act and introduced mandatory payment timelines that every owner, general contractor, and contractor on a construction project needs to follow.

The intent is straightforward: if you receive a proper invoice, you pay it on time. If you have a legitimate dispute, you say so in writing within a specific window. If neither happens, there are consequences - interest, adjudication, and potential liens.

But the timelines themselves are where most people get tripped up. There are different deadlines depending on where you sit in the payment chain, and missing one can cost you.

The payment timeline

Here is every deadline that matters, in order.

Day 0

Contractor submits a proper invoice to the owner

The clock starts when the owner receives a proper invoice - not when the work was completed, not when the invoice was dated. A proper invoice must include specific information required by the Act: who the parties are, a description of the work, the contract reference, the amount owing, and the payment terms. If the invoice is missing required elements, the owner can reject it and the clock doesn't start until a corrected invoice is received.

Within 14 days

Owner must send a notice of non-payment (if disputing)

If the owner disputes all or part of an invoice, they must deliver a written notice of non-payment within 14 days of receiving the proper invoice. The notice must state the amount not being paid and the reason. This is not optional. If you dispute an invoice but don't send the notice within 14 days, you've missed your window and the full amount is treated as owing.

Within 28 days

Owner must pay the contractor

The owner has 28 calendar days from receipt of the proper invoice to pay the undisputed amount. Not 28 business days - calendar days. If the invoice is $100,000 and the owner disputes $15,000 with a valid notice of non-payment, the remaining $85,000 must be paid within 28 days. No exceptions.

Within 7 days of receiving payment

Contractor must pay their contractors down the chain

Once a contractor receives payment from the owner, they have 7 days to pay their own contractors for work covered by that payment. This flow-down obligation continues at every tier. If you're a general contractor, you can't sit on funds meant for the trades below you.

28 / 14 / 7 The three numbers every Alberta contractor needs to know 28 days to pay. 14 days to dispute. 7 days to flow payment down the chain. Miss any of these and you're exposed - either to interest charges or to losing your right to dispute.

What happens when payment is late

If the owner doesn't pay the undisputed amount within 28 days, two things happen automatically:

Interest starts accruing. The rate is the Bank of Canada overnight rate plus 2%, calculated daily on the unpaid amount. This isn't negotiable and doesn't require a demand letter. It accrues by operation of the Act starting the day after the 28-day deadline passes.

To calculate it: check the current Bank of Canada overnight rate at bankofcanada.ca, add 2%, and apply that annual rate on a daily basis to the unpaid balance. If the overnight rate is 3.75%, late payment interest runs at 5.75% per year - roughly 0.016% per day on the outstanding amount.

The contractor can file for adjudication. This is a fast-track dispute resolution process created by the PPCLA. An adjudicator reviews the payment dispute and issues a binding interim decision, typically within 30 to 50 days. The decision is enforceable like a court order. What counts as a proper invoice

The payment clock only starts when the owner receives a proper invoice. If the invoice is missing required information, the owner can send it back and the 28-day timeline doesn't begin until a corrected version arrives.

Under the PPCLA, a proper invoice must include:

  • The contractor's name and address
  • The date of the invoice
  • A description of the work or materials covered
  • The contract or subcontract reference
  • The amount owing, including any holdback
  • The payment terms (net 28, unless the contract specifies otherwise within the PPCLA framework)
  • Any other information the contract requires for a proper invoice

That last point is important. Your contract can add requirements beyond what the Act specifies - purchase order numbers, cost codes, supporting timesheets, whatever the parties agree to. If those additional requirements are in the contract and the invoice doesn't meet them, it may not qualify as a proper invoice. For a deeper dive, see my guide on what makes a proper invoice under the PPCLA.

The dispute process

If you're an owner or general contractor and you have a legitimate reason not to pay all or part of an invoice, the PPCLA gives you a path - but you have to follow it precisely.

  1. Send a notice of non-payment within 14 days of receiving the proper invoice. The notice must be in writing, must state the amount being withheld, and must give the reason.
  2. Pay the undisputed portion within 28 days. You cannot hold the entire invoice because part of it is disputed. The undisputed amount still has to flow on time.
  3. Resolve the disputed amount. The parties can negotiate, but if they can't agree, either side can refer the dispute to adjudication.

If you skip step one - if you just don't pay and don't send a notice - you've lost your right to dispute under the Act. The full amount becomes payable, plus interest from day 29.

Public projects are different

Alberta government projects fall under the Public Works Act, not the PPCLA. The Public Works Act has its own prompt payment rules as of April 1, 2025. The timelines are similar but not identical, and the dispute mechanisms differ. If you're working on a provincial government project, make sure you're referencing the right legislation.

Municipal projects (cities, counties, school boards) generally fall under the PPCLA unless they've been specifically excluded.

What you should do now

If you're a contractor in Alberta:

  • Make sure your invoices qualify as proper invoices. If your invoice is missing required information, the payment clock isn't running and you may not realize it.
  • Track your deadlines. When you submit an invoice, note the date and calendar 28 days forward. If payment hasn't arrived, follow up on day 29 - not day 60.
  • Know your contract's additional requirements. Read the invoicing section of your MSA or subcontract. It may require PO numbers, cost code breakdowns, or other details that affect whether your invoice is considered proper.
  • Keep records of delivery. Send invoices by email with read receipts, or by registered mail. The 28-day clock starts on receipt, so you need to be able to prove when the invoice was received.

If you're an owner or general contractor:

  • Set up a system for the 14-day dispute window. When a proper invoice arrives, you have 14 days to send a notice of non-payment on any disputed portion. That's two weeks - and it goes fast when you're running a project.
  • Don't hold undisputed amounts. Even if part of an invoice is under dispute, pay the rest. The PPCLA doesn't allow you to hold the entire invoice while sorting out one line item.
  • Flow payments down within 7 days. When you receive payment from the owner, your contractors are due within 7 days. Build this into your AP process.
The PPCLA doesn't change what you owe. It changes how fast you owe it - and what happens when you're late.
