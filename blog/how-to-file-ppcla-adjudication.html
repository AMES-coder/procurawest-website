How to file for PPCLA adjudication in Alberta: step by step.

Amy-Marie Holland November 19, 2026 8 min read

You submitted a proper invoice. The 28-day payment deadline passed. The owner either didn't pay or sent a notice of non-payment that you believe is unjustified. Now what?

Under Alberta's Prompt Payment and Construction Lien Act (PPCLA), you don't have to sue. You can file for adjudication - a fast-track dispute resolution process designed specifically for construction payment disputes. It's faster than court, cheaper than litigation, and the decision is enforceable immediately.

Here's how the process works, from start to finish.

Who can file

Adjudication under the PPCLA is available to any party in the construction payment chain who has a payment dispute. That includes:

  • A contractor who hasn't been paid by the owner
  • A contractor who hasn't been paid by the general contractor
  • An owner who believes they overpaid or received deficient work
  • Any party who received a notice of non-payment they believe is unjustified

The dispute must relate to a construction contract governed by the PPCLA. If your project is a provincial government contract under the Public Works Act, the PPCLA adjudication process doesn't apply - that legislation has its own dispute resolution framework.

The process: 6 steps

Step 1

Send a Notice of Adjudication

Before you apply for an adjudicator, you must send a written Notice of Adjudication to the other party. This notice must describe: the nature of the dispute, the parties involved, the contract the dispute relates to, the amount claimed, and the remedy you're seeking. There's no prescribed form - it's a letter that sets out your claim clearly. Send it by registered mail or email with delivery confirmation so you have proof of receipt.

Step 2

Apply to a nominating authority

Within the timeframe set by the PPCLA (after sending your Notice of Adjudication), apply to a nominating authority to have an adjudicator appointed. Alberta's designated nominating authorities include the Alberta Dispute Resolution Institute (ADRI) and other bodies approved by the Alberta government. The application requires a copy of your Notice of Adjudication, a description of the dispute, and the application fee. Each authority publishes its own fee schedule - typically $350 to $750.

Step 3

Adjudicator is appointed

The nominating authority appoints a qualified adjudicator within 7 days of receiving your application. The adjudicator is an independent professional with construction industry and legal expertise. Neither party gets to choose the adjudicator - the nominating authority assigns one from their roster. If either party objects to the appointment, the grounds for objection are limited to conflicts of interest.

Step 4

Submit your documents

Within 5 days of the adjudicator's appointment, the claimant (the party who filed) must submit their documents. This is your case package: the contract, the invoice(s) in question, proof of delivery, any notice of non-payment received, correspondence, backup documentation (timesheets, supplier invoices, field tickets), and a written submission explaining your position. Be thorough - this is your main opportunity to present your case. The adjudicator will set a deadline for the respondent to reply.

Step 5

Respondent replies

The respondent submits their documents and position within the adjudicator's deadline. This is their opportunity to present their side - why the amount was disputed, what deficiencies they identified, what their notice of non-payment was based on. The adjudicator may allow one round of further submissions from each side, but this isn't a trial. There are no witnesses, no cross-examination, and no discovery process.

Step 6

Adjudicator issues a decision

The adjudicator has 30 days from receiving the claimant's documents to issue a written decision. The decision states the amount to be paid (if any), who pays the adjudicator's fees, and the timeline for compliance. This 30-day clock can be extended by agreement of both parties, but the adjudicator can't extend it unilaterally. Most decisions are issued within the 30 days.

30-50 days Typical timeline from filing to decision Compare that to 12-24 months for a construction lien action in court. Adjudication was designed to keep cash flowing on projects while disputes are resolved.

What happens after the decision

The adjudicator's decision is binding on an interim basis. That means:

The losing party must comply immediately. If the adjudicator orders payment, that amount is due within the timeline specified in the decision. Non-compliance can be enforced through the courts - you can register the decision and enforce it like a court judgment.

Either party can still go to court or arbitration. The adjudication decision isn't final in the same way a court judgment is. Either party can challenge it afterward through litigation or arbitration. But here's the key principle: pay now, argue later. You have to comply with the adjudicator's decision first. You can't refuse to pay while you litigate.

This is what makes PPCLA adjudication effective. Even if the dispute ultimately ends up in court, the contractor gets paid in the meantime. The cash flow keeps moving.

What it costs

Adjudication costs include three components:

  • Nominating authority application fee: $350 to $750, depending on the authority and the amount in dispute.
  • Adjudicator's fees: The adjudicator charges for their time reviewing documents and writing the decision. Rates vary, but expect $250 to $500 per hour. Most adjudications involve 10 to 30 hours of adjudicator time, depending on complexity.
  • Your own preparation costs: Time spent preparing your submission, organizing documents, and potentially getting legal advice on your position.

The adjudicator's fees are split equally between the parties unless the adjudicator decides otherwise in their decision. Some adjudicators allocate a greater share of costs to the unsuccessful party.

All-in, most adjudications cost each party between $2,000 and $8,000. That's a fraction of what a court action would cost - and you get a decision in weeks instead of years.

Tips for a strong filing

  • Start with a proper invoice. If your invoice wasn't a proper invoice under the PPCLA, the payment clock may never have started. Review the requirements before you file. See my guide on what makes a proper invoice under the PPCLA.
  • Organize your documents before you file. The 5-day submission window after the adjudicator is appointed goes fast. Have your contract, invoices, delivery records, correspondence, and backup documentation organized and ready before you send the Notice of Adjudication.
  • Be specific about the amount. Vague claims weaken your position. State exactly what was invoiced, what was paid, what's outstanding, and how you calculated interest on the late amount.
  • Include proof of proper invoice delivery. If the other side argues they never received a proper invoice, you need delivery evidence. Email read receipts, registered mail tracking, or courier confirmation.
  • Keep it factual. Adjudicators are experienced construction professionals. They respond to clear documentation and contractual references, not emotional arguments. Let the contract and the invoice trail speak for themselves.
Adjudication isn't adversarial in the way litigation is. It's a document-based process with a compressed timeline. The party with better documentation usually prevails.

When adjudication isn't the right path

Adjudication works well for straightforward payment disputes - invoices that weren't paid, amounts that were wrongly withheld, notice of non-payment defenses that don't hold up. It's less effective for disputes that involve complex technical questions about work quality, design deficiency claims, or multi-party liability issues that require expert evidence and cross-examination.

If your dispute is fundamentally about whether the work was done properly rather than whether the invoice was paid on time, litigation or arbitration may be a better forum. But even then, adjudication can get you an interim payment while the larger dispute is resolved.
