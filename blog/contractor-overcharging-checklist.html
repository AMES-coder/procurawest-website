How to check if your contractor is overcharging you.

Amy-Marie Holland November 19, 2026 6 min read

Most contractor overcharges are not fraud. They are billing mistakes, rate misapplications, and copy-paste errors that add up quietly over the life of a project. The problem is that most companies approve invoices by checking the total against their budget rather than auditing the line items against the contract.

I have reviewed hundreds of contractor invoices across oil and gas, construction, and infrastructure projects. The same errors show up again and again. This checklist walks you through the exact steps I use to catch them before payment goes out.

3-8% Typical Overpayment Rate Companies without a formal invoice audit process overpay by 3 to 8 percent on average. On a $2 million contract, that is $60,000 to $160,000 walking out the door unchecked.

Step 1: Pull the contract rate schedule

Before you even open the invoice, pull the original contract and find the rate schedule. This is your baseline. Every rate on the invoice needs to trace back to an agreed number in the contract.

What you are looking for:

  • Labour rates by classification - journeyman, apprentice, foreman, superintendent. Each should have a defined hourly or daily rate.
  • Equipment rates - are they hourly, daily, or monthly? Is fuel included or billed separately?
  • Material markup percentages - most contracts allow a 10-15% markup on materials. Some contractors apply higher markups when no one checks.
  • Escalation clauses - if the contract is more than a year old, check whether annual rate increases have been applied correctly.

If you do not have a rate schedule in the contract, that is your first problem. You are paying whatever the contractor decides to bill.

Step 2: Run a three-way match

The three-way match is the backbone of invoice auditing. You compare three documents: the purchase order (what you authorized), the contract (what you agreed to pay), and the invoice (what the contractor is billing). All three need to agree.

Check these specifically:

  • Does the invoice reference the correct PO number?
  • Do the rates on the invoice match the contract rate schedule?
  • Do the quantities or hours fall within the PO limits?
  • Has the contractor billed for work outside the original scope without an approved change order?

If anything does not match, do not approve the invoice. Send it back with a written explanation of the discrepancy and ask for a revised submission.

Step 3: Cross-reference hours against site logs

Labour is where most overcharges hide. A contractor bills 10 hours for a crew of four, but the site log shows them arriving at 8:00 AM and leaving at 3:30 PM. That is 7.5 hours, not 10.

What to compare:

  • Invoiced hours vs. supervisor-signed timesheets - if your site supervisor did not sign off on the hours, ask why not.
  • Crew size vs. billed headcount - check that the number of workers billed matches who was actually on site.
  • Worker classifications - a journeyman rate for apprentice-level work is a common billing error.
  • Overtime justification - was overtime authorized in advance? Check your contract for overtime approval requirements.
If your site does not keep daily logs or require signed timesheets, start tomorrow. You cannot audit what you did not record.

Step 4: Audit material markups

Your contract likely allows the contractor to pass through material costs plus a markup - typically 10 to 15 percent. The question is whether the base cost being marked up is accurate.

Here is what to check:

  • Request supplier invoices or receipts for any material charge over your threshold (I recommend $500).
  • Compare the contractor's material cost against current supplier pricing or your own vendor quotes.
  • Check the markup percentage - is it being applied to the net cost or the gross cost including tax?
  • Watch for bundled materials - some contractors include consumables (welding rod, fasteners, PPE) that should be covered under their base rate.

On larger projects, I recommend running a spot audit on 10-20% of material line items each month rather than trying to verify everything. The pattern will tell you whether deeper review is needed.

Step 5: Spot duplicate charges

Duplicate charges are more common than you would expect, especially on long-running projects with monthly billing cycles. They happen when a contractor bills for the same work in consecutive invoices, or when a progress claim includes items that were already paid.

Red flags to look for:

  • The same line item description appearing in back-to-back invoices.
  • Mobilization or demobilization fees billed more than once.
  • Lump-sum items reappearing after they were already paid in full.
  • Progress percentages that jump backward - for example, electrical rough-in billed at 80% one month and then 60% the next, which may indicate a rebill.

Keep a running tracker of cumulative amounts billed against each contract line item. A simple spreadsheet works. Without it, duplicates slip through. For more patterns like these, see my post on contractor invoice red flags.

Step 6: Review mobilization and special fees

Mobilization fees, small tools allowances, safety charges, and administrative fees are where vague contract language gets expensive. If the contract says "mobilization as required" without defining a rate, you will see creative billing.

Questions to ask:

  • Is the mobilization fee defined in the contract, or is it being billed at the contractor's discretion?
  • Are safety costs included in the hourly rate, or are they a legitimate separate charge?
  • Is the contractor charging an administrative fee that was not in the original agreement?
  • Are per diem and travel costs within the ranges specified in the contract?

Build the habit

Invoice auditing is not a one-time exercise. It is a process that needs to happen on every payment cycle. The first time you catch a $15,000 billing error, the value of a systematic review becomes obvious.

If you do not have the internal capacity to audit every invoice, focus on the highest-risk contracts first - T&M agreements, contracts without defined rate schedules, and any contractor billing more than $50,000 per month. Those are where the biggest savings hide.
