How to build a procurement budget for a construction project.

Amy-Marie Holland November 19, 2026 7 min read

Every construction project starts with a budget. But in my experience working with Alberta owners and general contractors, the procurement budget is either missing entirely or lumped into a single line item that says "materials and contracts." That's not a budget. That's a guess.

A proper procurement budget breaks down every cost category you'll encounter when buying materials, hiring contractors, renting equipment, and managing the commercial side of a construction project. It tells you what you're going to spend, when you're going to spend it, and where you're most likely to go over.

Here's how I build one.

The core cost categories

Every construction procurement budget in Alberta needs these categories at minimum:

Direct materials

This is the physical stuff - steel, concrete, lumber, pipe, electrical, mechanical components. For each material package, your budget should include the base material cost, delivery and freight charges, applicable taxes, and any storage or staging costs if materials arrive before the site is ready to receive them.

In Alberta, material costs can fluctuate significantly depending on the season and global supply chains. Steel prices in particular have been volatile over the past several years. I always recommend getting firm quotes with validity periods rather than using catalogue pricing in your budget.

Labour - contracted services

This covers every contractor you'll engage - general trades, mechanical, electrical, instrumentation, civil, structural, insulation, painting, scaffolding. For each trade package, budget the contract value plus a contingency for change orders.

If you're using time and materials contracts, your budget should reflect the estimated hours and rates with a not-to-exceed cap. If you're using lump sum contracts, budget the awarded amount plus 5% to 10% for anticipated changes. Very few construction contracts close out at exactly the original contract value.

Equipment

Both owned and rented equipment. Cranes, excavators, compressors, generators, scaffolding systems, temporary power. Don't forget mobilization and demobilization costs, which can be substantial for heavy equipment. A crawler crane mobilization in Alberta can run $15,000 to $30,000 each way depending on the distance and road conditions.

Insurance and bonding

Your procurement budget needs to account for:

  • Builder's risk insurance - covers the project during construction against fire, weather, theft, and other specified perils
  • Commercial general liability - required from every contractor, typically $2 million to $5 million per occurrence in Alberta
  • Performance and payment bonds - if required by your contract or by the project owner, typically 50% of the contract value for each bond, with premiums running 1% to 3% of the bond amount
  • Professional liability - for any design or engineering work

Bonding costs are often overlooked in preliminary budgets. On a $10 million project requiring 50% performance and payment bonds, bonding premiums alone can be $100,000 to $300,000.

Permits and regulatory fees

In Alberta, construction permits are issued through local municipalities under the Alberta Safety Codes Act. Budget for:

  • Building permits (typically calculated as a percentage of construction value)
  • Electrical, plumbing, gas, and mechanical permits (separate from the building permit)
  • Development permits if the project involves a change in land use
  • Environmental permits - particularly for projects near water bodies, wetlands, or on previously contaminated land
  • Alberta Energy Regulator fees if the project involves oil and gas infrastructure

Professional services

Engineering, surveying, geotechnical investigation, environmental assessment, materials testing, commissioning support. These are procurement costs that belong in your budget, not hidden in the project management overhead. On a mid-size Alberta construction project, professional services can run 8% to 12% of the total hard costs.

Alberta-specific costs most budgets miss

These are the line items that trip up out-of-province companies and first-time Alberta project owners:

  • WCB (Workers' Compensation Board) premiums. Every contractor working in Alberta must have WCB coverage. Premium rates vary by industry code - construction trades range from roughly $1.50 to over $8.00 per $100 of assessable earnings depending on the trade and the contractor's safety record. As the project owner, you need to verify WCB clearance letters from every contractor before they start work, and your budget should reflect the WCB cost component that contractors build into their rates.
  • Environmental compliance. Alberta has specific requirements for erosion and sediment control, waste management, and environmental monitoring during construction. If your project triggers an Environmental Impact Assessment or requires an EPEA (Environmental Protection and Enhancement Act) approval, the costs can be significant - $50,000 to $200,000 or more depending on the project.
  • Cold weather construction. If your project runs through an Alberta winter, budget for temporary heating, hoarding, concrete blankets, and reduced productivity. Labour productivity in extreme cold can drop 15% to 30% depending on the trade and the temperature. Concrete pours below -10 degrees Celsius require heated enclosures and extended curing protection.
  • PPCLA holdback. Under Alberta's Prompt Payment and Construction Lien Act, you're required to hold back 10% of each progress payment until the lien period expires. This holdback needs to be reflected in your cash flow plan. It's not an additional cost - you'll release it eventually - but it affects your financing requirements and your contractors' cash flow expectations.

Common budget overruns

After working on dozens of Alberta construction projects, I can tell you where budgets consistently blow up:

  • Change orders. The number one source of budget overruns in construction. Design changes, unforeseen site conditions, owner-requested additions. Budget 5% to 10% above your base contract values for changes, even on well-defined projects.
  • Delivery and logistics. Especially on remote Alberta projects - oil sands, pipelines, rural infrastructure. Freight costs for heavy or oversized loads, road bans during spring breakup, and seasonal access restrictions can double your material delivery costs.
  • Schedule delays. When the schedule slips, indirect costs keep running - site supervision, temporary facilities, equipment standby. A one-month delay on a mid-size project can add $50,000 to $150,000 in procurement-related indirect costs.
  • Scope gaps between trade packages. The mechanical contractor assumes the general contractor is providing temporary power. The general contractor assumes the electrical contractor is handling it. Nobody budgeted for it. These scope gaps between trade packages are a consistent source of overruns that good procurement planning prevents.

Contingency planning

Your contingency is not a slush fund. It's a managed reserve with clear rules for how it gets spent.

I recommend:

  • 5% to 10% contingency for well-defined projects with complete design and established contractors
  • 10% to 15% contingency for projects with partial design, new contractors, or environmental unknowns
  • Approval authority defined in advance - who can authorize contingency draws, at what dollar threshold, and what documentation is required
  • Monthly tracking of contingency balance and burn rate so you can see early if you're heading for a problem

Keep the contingency as a separate line item in your budget. When it gets buried in the trade packages, nobody tracks it and nobody knows when it's gone until the invoices are already in.

A procurement budget isn't a spreadsheet exercise. It's the financial blueprint for how your project gets built.
