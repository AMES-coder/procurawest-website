What to do if your contractor isn't paying you in Alberta.

Amy-Marie Holland November 19, 2026 7 min read

You did the work. You sent the invoice. And now it's been 30, 60, maybe 90 days and you still haven't been paid. If you're a contractor working on a construction project in Alberta, you're not powerless. The law gives you specific rights and specific timelines to recover what you're owed - but you have to act within those timelines or you lose your options.

Here's what to do, step by step.

Step 1: Confirm your invoice is proper

Before you escalate anything, make sure your invoice meets the requirements of a "proper invoice" under Alberta's Prompt Payment and Construction Lien Act (PPCLA). This matters because the payment clock - the 28-day deadline for the owner to pay - doesn't start until a proper invoice is received.

A proper invoice must include:

  • Your name and address
  • The date of the invoice
  • A description of the work or materials
  • The contract or agreement reference
  • The amount owing, including any holdback
  • Payment terms
  • Any additional information your contract requires

If your invoice is missing any of these elements, the party you're invoicing can reject it. The 28-day clock doesn't start until you send a corrected version. I've seen contractors wait months for payment only to discover their invoice was never accepted as proper because it was missing a purchase order number. For a full breakdown, see my guide on PPCLA payment deadlines.

Step 2: Know your PPCLA rights

Under the PPCLA, the payment chain works like this:

28 days

Owner pays the general contractor

The owner has 28 calendar days from receipt of a proper invoice to pay the undisputed amount. If they dispute part of it, they must send a written notice of non-payment within 14 days specifying the amount and reason.

7 days after receiving payment

General contractor pays their contractors

Once the general contractor receives payment from the owner, they have 7 days to flow payment down to their contractors. This obligation continues at every tier of the payment chain.

Automatic

Interest accrues on late payments

If payment is late, interest accrues at the Bank of Canada overnight rate plus 2%, calculated daily. This happens automatically by operation of the Act - no demand letter required.

If the contractor above you has been paid by the owner and hasn't paid you within 7 days, they're in breach of the PPCLA. That gives you grounds for adjudication and interest on the outstanding amount.

45 days Your window to register a builders' lien in Alberta You have 45 days from your last day of furnishing labour, materials, or equipment to the project to register a lien at the Land Titles Office. Miss this deadline and you lose your lien rights entirely. Mark it on your calendar the day you finish work.

Step 3: Send a demand letter

If payment is overdue and your phone calls and emails aren't getting results, it's time for a formal demand letter. A demand letter is a written notice that states the amount owing, the basis for the debt (the contract and the work performed), the PPCLA interest accruing, and a deadline for payment - typically 7 to 14 days.

Your demand letter should include:

  • The contract or purchase order reference
  • The invoice number and date
  • The amount owing, including PPCLA interest calculated to date
  • A clear statement that the amount is overdue under the PPCLA
  • A deadline for payment (7 to 14 days from receipt)
  • A statement that you will pursue adjudication and/or register a builders' lien if payment is not received

Send it by email and registered mail. Keep copies of everything. The demand letter serves two purposes: it often prompts payment from parties who are simply slow or disorganized, and it creates a paper trail that strengthens your position if you proceed to adjudication or litigation.

Step 4: Consider adjudication

The PPCLA created a fast-track dispute resolution process called adjudication. It's designed to resolve payment disputes quickly - typically within 30 to 50 days - without the cost and delay of going to court.

Here's how it works:

  1. You file a notice of adjudication with an authorized nominating authority (currently the Alberta Dispute Resolution Society). The filing fee is typically a few hundred dollars.
  2. An adjudicator is appointed within a set timeframe. The adjudicator is an independent professional with construction industry experience.
  3. Both parties submit their positions in writing, with supporting documents - invoices, contracts, correspondence, timesheets, whatever supports the claim or defense.
  4. The adjudicator issues a decision that is binding on an interim basis. The losing party must comply with the decision, including paying the amount determined to be owing.

The adjudicator's decision is enforceable like a court order. If the other party doesn't comply, you can register the decision with the Court of King's Bench and enforce it through the courts. Either party can later challenge the decision through litigation or arbitration, but they have to comply with it in the meantime.

Adjudication is particularly effective for straightforward payment disputes where the work was done, the invoice was proper, and the other side simply isn't paying. It's less suited for complex technical disputes about quality or scope, though the adjudicator can still make interim determinations on those issues.

Step 5: Protect your lien rights

Separate from the PPCLA adjudication process, you have the right to register a builders' lien against the property where you performed the work. A lien is a powerful tool - it attaches to the land title and must be dealt with before the property can be sold or refinanced.

The critical timelines for builders' liens in Alberta:

  • 45 days to register. You must register your lien at the Land Titles Office within 45 days of your last day of furnishing labour, materials, or equipment to the project. This is not 45 business days - it's 45 calendar days. Miss it and you're done.
  • 180 days to start an action. Once your lien is registered, you have 180 days to start a court action to enforce it. If you don't start the action within 180 days, the lien expires.
  • The lien amount includes holdback. If the owner has been holding back 10% of progress payments as required by the PPCLA, that holdback is part of the lien fund available to lien claimants.

Registering a lien typically requires a lawyer. The registration itself involves a specific form filed with the Land Titles Office, and mistakes in the registration can invalidate the lien. This is one area where I always recommend getting legal advice rather than trying to do it yourself.

Step 6: Know when to involve a lawyer

You don't necessarily need a lawyer for every payment dispute. A demand letter, a PPCLA adjudication filing, and persistent follow-up can resolve many situations. But there are circumstances where legal representation becomes important:

  • The amount is large. If you're owed $50,000 or more, the stakes justify legal costs. For smaller amounts, adjudication may be more cost-effective than litigation.
  • You need to register a lien. Lien registration has technical requirements, and errors can void the lien. A construction lawyer can file it properly.
  • The other party has a lawyer. If you receive a legal response to your demand letter or if the other side files a counterclaim during adjudication, get your own counsel.
  • The dispute involves scope or quality. Payment disputes that are really about whether the work was done properly, or whether additional work was authorized, can be more complex than a straightforward "pay the invoice" claim.
  • Insolvency is a risk. If the contractor above you is showing signs of financial distress - paying late across the board, losing other contracts, laying off staff - acting quickly through legal channels may be the difference between recovering your money and writing it off.
