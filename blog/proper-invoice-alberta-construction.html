What makes a proper invoice under Alberta's PPCLA?

Amy-Marie Holland November 19, 2026 6 min read

Under Alberta's Prompt Payment and Construction Lien Act (PPCLA), the 28-day payment clock starts when the owner receives a proper invoice. Not when the work is finished. Not when the invoice is dated. And not when any invoice arrives - it has to be a proper invoice.

That distinction matters more than most contractors realize. If your invoice is missing a required element, the owner can send it back and the payment clock never starts. You could be waiting 60 or 90 days for payment and have no PPCLA recourse because, technically, you never submitted a proper invoice.

Here's exactly what the Act requires - and what your contract might add on top of it.

The PPCLA requirements

A proper invoice under the PPCLA must contain the following information. If any of these is missing, the invoice can be rejected and the clock resets to zero when you resubmit.

Required

Contractor's name and address

Your legal business name and mailing address. If you operate under a trade name, include both the trade name and the legal entity name. The owner needs to be able to identify who they're paying and where to send the cheque or EFT confirmation.

Required

Date of the invoice

The date the invoice was issued. This isn't the date the work was performed - it's the date you're billing. The payment clock starts on the date of receipt, not the invoice date, but the invoice date is still a required field.

Required

Description of the work or materials

A clear description of what you're billing for. "Services rendered" isn't sufficient. The description should be specific enough that the owner can match it against the scope of work in the contract. For example: "Electrical rough-in, Building A, floors 2-4, per SOW Section 3.2" is specific. "Electrical work - October" is not.

Required

Contract or subcontract reference

The contract number, subcontract number, or purchase order number that the work was performed under. This ties the invoice to a specific agreement. If there are multiple contracts or change orders, reference each one separately with the corresponding amount.

Required

Amount owing, including holdback

The total amount of the invoice, broken down to show the gross amount, any statutory holdback being retained by the owner, and the net amount due. Under the PPCLA, the statutory holdback is 10% of the value of work done or materials furnished. Show it on the invoice even if the owner is the one retaining it - it demonstrates you understand the holdback obligation and prevents disputes about whether the invoiced amount includes or excludes holdback.

Required

Payment terms

The terms of payment, including the due date. Under the PPCLA, payment is due within 28 calendar days unless the contract specifies a shorter period (a contract cannot extend beyond 28 days for the owner-to-contractor payment). State "Net 28 days" or the specific terms your contract provides.

Required

Any additional information required by the contract

This is the catch-all - and it's the one that trips up most contractors. Your contract can specify additional invoicing requirements beyond what the Act requires. If the contract says your invoice must include a purchase order number, cost code breakdown, WBS reference, site supervisor sign-off, or attached timesheets, then those are part of what makes the invoice "proper" under your specific contract.

Contract additions: the fine print

The PPCLA sets the floor for what a proper invoice must contain. Your contract sets the ceiling. Here are common additions I see in Alberta construction and services contracts:

  • Purchase order number - Almost universal on corporate contracts. If the PO number is wrong or missing, AP will bounce the invoice.
  • Cost code or WBS breakdown - The owner wants to see charges allocated to specific cost codes so they can track spending against their budget. If your invoice is one lump sum with no cost breakdown, it may not qualify as proper.
  • Timesheets or daily logs - For T&M (time and materials) or reimbursable contracts, the supporting timesheets are often a contractual requirement for a proper invoice. No timesheets attached = no proper invoice.
  • Progress photos or completion certificates - Some contracts, particularly milestone-based ones, require evidence of completion before an invoice is considered proper.
  • Statutory declarations - Some owners require a statutory declaration that all contractors and suppliers have been paid for prior invoicing periods. This is separate from the lien holdback.
  • GST registration number - Required for GST purposes and often a contractual invoicing requirement as well.
  • Schedule of rates reference - If your contract includes a rate schedule, the invoice should reference the applicable rates and show how the billed amounts tie back to contracted rates.

Read your contract's invoicing section carefully - ideally before you submit your first invoice, not after it gets rejected.

Common mistakes that kill the payment clock

These are the errors I see most often when reviewing contractor invoicing processes:

Missing the contract reference. You'd be surprised how many invoices go out with no contract number, PO number, or scope reference. The billing team is working from a template and doesn't have the contract details loaded. Fix: build a billing template for each active contract with the reference fields pre-populated.

Sending invoices to the wrong person. The contract specifies that invoices must be sent to a specific email address or AP department. If you send it to the project manager instead, the owner can argue they never received a proper invoice at the designated address. Fix: check the contract's "Notices" section for the correct invoicing address.

Lumping multiple contracts onto one invoice. If you have three active contracts with the same owner, don't combine them on one invoice unless the contract specifically allows it. Each contract should have its own invoice with its own contract reference. Combining them creates disputes about which contract the payment applies to.

Not showing holdback. Even if the owner is the one retaining holdback, your invoice should show the gross amount, the holdback deduction, and the net amount payable. If you invoice $100,000 without mentioning holdback, and the owner pays $90,000 (after retaining 10% holdback), you've created a dispute about whether $10,000 is outstanding.

Vague work descriptions. "Professional services - November 2026" tells the owner nothing about what they're paying for. It makes it easy for them to dispute the invoice and hard for you to prove the work was done. Be specific: what scope, what location, what deliverables, what period.

A proper invoice checklist

Before you submit any invoice on a PPCLA-governed project, run through this:

  1. Is your legal business name and address on the invoice?
  2. Is the invoice dated?
  3. Does the work description match the contract scope?
  4. Is the correct contract number, PO number, or subcontract reference included?
  5. Is the amount broken down showing gross, holdback, and net?
  6. Are payment terms stated (Net 28 or per contract)?
  7. Have you included every additional item your contract requires (timesheets, cost codes, stat decs, etc.)?
  8. Is the invoice addressed to the correct person or department per the contract's notice provisions?
  9. Do you have proof of delivery (email read receipt, registered mail tracking)?

If the answer to any of these is no, fix it before you send. A rejected invoice means the payment clock starts over.

The best invoice in the world is worthless if the owner can argue it wasn't a proper invoice. Get the form right, and the PPCLA does the rest.
