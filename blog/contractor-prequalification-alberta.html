Contractor prequalification without the chaos.

Amy-Marie Holland July 20, 2026 7 min read

If you're running contracts in oil and gas or construction in Alberta, you already know that contractor prequalification is not optional. Your clients require it. Your insurance requires it. Your COR certification requires it. The question isn't whether to do it - it's how to do it without it becoming a second full-time job for your team.

What nobody talks about publicly is what prequalification management looks like from the hiring client side. Everyone writes about ISN and Avetta from the contractor's perspective - the fees, the renewals, the paperwork. Very little has been written about the burden on the companies managing the programs.

That's where I spend most of my time.

The four platforms and what they're actually for

Most Alberta operators and general contractors are working across two or three of these simultaneously:

ISNetworld (ISN)

Dominant in oil & gas

The standard for SAGD operators, midstream companies, and major energy clients. Heavy document requirements. Annual renewal cycles. Your sub-contractors are paying an annual subscription to maintain their profile.

Avetta

Broad cross-sector use

Strong in construction, utilities, and industrial. More flexible than ISN in some respects, but still requires active management from the hiring client side. Subscribers pay annually. You're responsible for reviewing their submissions.

ComplyWorks

Canadian-focused compliance

Calgary-founded, now part of Veriforce. Built for the Canadian market. Strong WCB and COR integration. Used heavily by larger GCs and energy companies with complex compliance matrices across multiple provinces.

ContractorCheck

Compliance verification focus

Now part of Alcumus. Document collection and insurance verification. Popular with ICI construction in Alberta. Lower fees for contractors than ISN, which can improve vendor participation rates.

If your company is on two or three of these platforms, you're managing different renewal dates, different document requirements, and different reporting dashboards - all at once. That's the practical reality of running a hiring client program in Alberta.

What actually falls through the cracks

When there's no dedicated person managing the hiring client side of prequalification, here's what consistently goes wrong:

What I find when I audit a new client's compliance program

  • A compliance tracking spreadsheet that's 4–6 months behind. Renewal dates missed. Expired documents on file. No one knows who's current.
  • A contractor working on site whose ISN account lapsed 3 weeks ago. The platform flagged it. Nobody saw the flag.
  • WCB clearance letters that only cover work up to their issue date. The letter on file is 6 months old and was never refreshed before payments went out.
  • An audit request from a client revealing that a big chunk of your active vendor list has compliance gaps - some of them critical.
  • Insurance certificates that don't match your contract requirements (wrong limits, missing additional insureds) that got approved at renewal without a proper review.
  • A sub-trade who changed their legal entity name after an acquisition and is now technically un-prequalified under their new name, but still working under the old approval.

None of this is the contractor's fault. The platforms are doing their job - flagging expiries, requesting renewals, tracking document status. The problem is that someone on your side has to act on those flags. If that someone is your project coordinator who's also doing three other things, the flags go unread.

The cost of getting it wrong

Contractor compliance is one of the few areas in procurement where the downside isn't financial - it's operational and legal.

If a contractor gets injured on site with a lapsed WCB account, you may be on the hook for their unpaid WCB premiums - WCB-Alberta can hold the hiring company responsible if you paid without a current clearance letter. Your insurance may not cover it if your own compliance process was deficient.

If a client audits your vendor compliance and finds gaps, you're looking at potential disqualification from future work - or at minimum, an uncomfortable conversation about whether you actually manage your supply chain the way you said you do.

If you have a COR certification, contractor compliance is part of the audit criteria. Gaps in your vendor compliance records can put your COR status at risk. In sectors where COR is a bid requirement, that's not a small problem.

If something goes wrong on a job site and it comes out that the contractor wasn't properly prequalified, your liability exposure in a legal dispute is substantially higher than if your compliance records are clean.

The platforms are tools. The management is the job. Most companies have the tool and nobody doing the job.

What a functioning program looks like

A well-run contractor prequalification program isn't complicated. It's consistent. Here's what it includes:

An approved vendor list with status tracking. Every contractor on your approved list has a status: active (all requirements current), expiring within 30 days (renewal initiated), suspended (working on compliance), or removed. Status is updated weekly. Someone owns it.

Automated expiry alerts with a decision protocol. The platforms all have alert functionality - you just have to configure it and make sure someone acts on it. Pull a fresh WCB clearance letter at hire and before each payment. Set a 60-day lead on ISN renewals. When an alert fires, it goes to a named person with a deadline.

Insurance certificate reviews at onboarding and renewal. Not just collecting the certificate. Reading it. Does the limit match the contract requirement? Is your company named as an additional insured? Is the policy in the right entity name? This takes five minutes per certificate and prevents significant exposure.

Quarterly compliance reports for your internal stakeholders. How many vendors are active? How many are expiring? What's the open-items list? Your operations managers and project teams should know the compliance status of vendors they're working with. It shouldn't come as a surprise.

A documented onboarding process for new vendors. What do you require before a new contractor can be mobilized? Who reviews their submissions? How long does approval take? This process should be written down and consistently applied. Not every project manager doing it differently.

The honest conversation about platform fees

Your contractors have opinions about ISN and Avetta subscription fees. They can be paying thousands of dollars a year across multiple platforms to stay visible in your system, and they pass some of that cost back to you in their rates. That's a real dynamic.

What I tell clients: you can't control what the platforms charge. You can control whether you're actually extracting value from the data they collect. If you're paying for Avetta and nobody on your side is checking the compliance dashboard, you're spending the contractor's money (and yours, indirectly) for a database you're not using.

The platforms work when someone is managing them. That's the whole point.
